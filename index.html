WIUT Hackathon 2026 · FinTech / AI in Finance · AML alert prioritization

Big money is not the warning sign. Small transfers are.

A bank's monitoring team gets more alerts than it can check. We studied 14,000 past alerts and the 7 million payments behind them to learn which alerts should be checked first, and built a model that does this sorting.

Team Ufq · Team ID 1B8F4334 · Exploratory data analysis for the elimination task

Key findings

  1. 17 of every 100 alerts are serious. The other 83 are false alarms, so the order in which a team checks alerts matters.
  2. Timing and activity do not tell them apart. Serious and false-alarm customers pay at the same times and only a little more often (6%).
  3. Payment size does. Customers with the smallest bank transfers were serious almost twice as often as those with the largest: 23% against 12%.
  4. Our model turns this into a better order. If a team can check 30 of every 100 alerts, it finds 8 of the 17 serious ones with our model and 5 without it.

1 · The problem

Only 17 of every 100 alerts are serious

Banks use software that watches every payment. When a customer looks unusual, the software raises an alert. A specialist then reviews the alert and makes one of two decisions:

  • Serious: send it for a full investigation (in the data: escalated).
  • False alarm: close it (in the data: dismissed).

This matters for the bank itself. The money being hidden is not the bank's money, but a bank that lets it pass can face large fines and even lose its license. Specialists cannot study every alert deeply, so the order in which they check alerts is important.

The share of serious alerts stays almost the same over time: between 16% and 19% in every three-month period of 2025–2026. So the date of an alert tells us very little.

For the model

We do not ask the model for a yes-or-no answer. We ask for a chance between 0 and 1 and sort the alerts by it. A model that calls every alert a false alarm would be right 83% of the time and still catch no one. The competition's score (ROC-AUC) also checks only the order.

So what makes the 17 different from the other 83? Everything we know about them is in their payments.

Each alert has the customer's payments from the 180 days before it: 499 on average, from 1 to 2,279. Alerts come from 2025 and 2026. The organizers created this data with a computer, so it contains no real customers. It is also clean: no missing values, no repeated IDs, and no payment belongs to two alerts. Four types in two directions give eight kinds of payments.

Card payments are by far the most common kind. Cash and international payments are rare: about 34 of the roughly 500 payments. Serious customers make a few more payments of every kind: 524 against 494 in total, or 6% more. But the mix is almost the same, so counting payments alone cannot separate the two groups.

For the model

We count the payments of each kind separately, and add each kind's share of all payments.

Serious customers are only a little busier. Maybe the difference is in when they pay?

3 · Before the alert

The months before an alert look the same for everyone

We followed every customer week by week through the six months before the alert.

Two things change for everyone as the alert gets closer. Customers pay less often: about 3 payments a day five months before, about 1 a day in the last week. And their payments get smaller. Serious customers follow the same path. They are simply 3–8% busier in every single week: a steady difference, not a sudden change before the alert.

A strange last day

41payments per customer in the last day before the alert
8%of all payments in the data fall on this one day
97%of these are made between 23:57 and 23:59

This looked important at first. But serious and false-alarm customers have almost the same number of these payments (42 and 40), so they do not help to tell them apart. We also noticed that on all other days, payments are spread perfectly evenly across the 24 hours: every hour has 4.2% of them. Both facts suggest this is how the data was prepared, not how people really behave.

For the model

We describe each alert's activity in the last 7, 30, 90 and 180 days, and in each 30-day block.

We also make one important fix. Payments get smaller for everyone as the alert gets closer. So we compare each payment with the average payment made on the same day before an alert, like comparing a child's height only with children of the same age. Now "small" and "big" mean the same thing, whenever the payment was made.

Timing does not separate the groups. So we looked at the last thing: how big the payments are, kind by kind.

4 · Payment sizes

The surprise: the smaller the transfers, the higher the risk

We expected serious customers to move larger amounts. To check, we sorted customers by the usual size of their outgoing bank transfers, split them into ten equal groups, and counted how many alerts in each group were serious.

In the group with the smallest transfers, 23 of every 100 alerts were serious. In the group with the largest, only 12.

Why could this be? Large transfers attract attention, and banks must report them. A person who wants to hide money can split one large amount into many small transfers that look normal one by one.

The same comparison works for every size of payment. Choose a kind of payment to see which sizes are more common among serious customers:

Put together, a typical serious customer has many small bank transfers, some large cash deposits, and card spending that jumps between very small and very large amounts. Each sign is weak alone, but they add up.

We also checked classic warning signs that banks look for: money that leaves within a day of arriving, many payments of almost the same amount, and payments at night or on weekends. None of them separated the two groups, so the model mostly uses sizes.

For the model

For each of the eight kinds of payments, we describe the sizes: the smallest, the middle and the largest ones, the average, the total and how much they vary, all measured after the fix from Section 3. We also compare the size of a customer's bank transfers with their own card payments. In total, every alert is described by 208 clues (the model's features).

Each clue is weak on its own. The model's job is to combine them.

5 · The model

A simple scorecard that learned from 14,000 decisions

Our model works like a scorecard (its technical name is logistic regression). Every clue adds or removes a few points, and the total is turned into a chance between 0% and 100%. Before scoring, each clue is measured in the same way (how far this customer is from a typical one), so no clue wins just because its numbers are big.

Here is the scorecard for two real past alerts:

Which clues count the most? The largest cash deposits and how much a customer's card spending varies push an alert up the list. Large bank transfers push it down. This is the same story as in Section 4.

How did it learn the points? We gave it the 14,000 past alerts together with the specialists' decisions. It started with a guess, measured how far its chances were from the real decisions, and corrected the points again and again until they matched as closely as possible. We also told it to be careful: a clue gets many points only if the data strongly supports it. Without this care, the test score drops from 0.640 to 0.627.

How did we test it fairly? Like an exam with hidden questions. We split the alerts into five groups, taught the model on four and tested it on the fifth, which it had never seen. We did this five times, so every alert was tested once. With six different random splits, the score stayed between 0.640 and 0.643, so the result is stable.

What does 0.640 mean? Take one serious alert and one false alarm at random. Our model puts the serious one higher 64 times out of 100; guessing would do it 50 times. That sounds modest, but the researchers who designed this kind of data reported 0.641 for their best model.

Why a simple model? We also tried boosted trees, a more complex model that many teams use. With the same 208 clues, its best version scored 0.633. The differences between serious and false-alarm customers are small, and a complex model starts to learn accidents that do not repeat in new data. The simpler we made the trees, the better they scored, but they never beat the simple model.

A score of 0.640 is still abstract. Here is what it means for a team with limited time.

6 · The result

Same work, more serious cases found

A simple example

A doctor has 100 patients waiting. 17 of them are seriously ill, but nobody knows which. There is time to see only 30 today.

Without a nurseThe doctor sees patients in the order they arrived. About 5 of the 30 are seriously ill.
With a nurseA nurse looks at everyone first and sends the sickest-looking patients in first. Now about 8 of the 30 are seriously ill.

The nurse is sometimes wrong, but the doctor sees more sick patients in the same time. In our case, the patients are alerts, the doctor is the bank's specialist, and the nurse is our model.

Here is the same idea with our real results. Take the 100 alerts from Section 1 and choose how many the team can check. The team checks the squares row by row, starting at the top.

If the team could check all 100 alerts, both ways would find all 17. The order matters only when time is short, and in a real bank it always is.

Across all 14,000 past alerts, the first tenth of our queue had 29% serious alerts, and the last tenth only 9%. Like the nurse, the model is sometimes wrong, but it moves most serious alerts toward the front.

Conclusion

What we learned

  • The difference is small but steady. Serious and false-alarm customers pay at the same times and almost as often. What differs is the size of their payments: smaller bank transfers, larger cash deposits and more uneven card spending, through all six months.
  • Two decisions mattered most. Describing each kind of payment separately, and comparing sizes fairly after removing the common change before the alert, raised our score from 0.562 to 0.640.
  • Simple beat complex. A careful scorecard model did better than boosted trees and matches the best published result for this kind of data.
  • It helps in practice. With the same work, a team that checks alerts in our order finds more serious cases.

What we would do next

Our model sees only payments. A real bank also knows who the customer is and what they do, and the same behavior can mean different things: many small payments from different people are normal for a freelancer but unusual for a student. Adding a little of this context could make the model much better, as long as it is used carefully and legally, and people are judged by what they do, never by who they are.

Notes on the data
  • The organizers state that the data is synthetic (computer-made) and contains no real customer records.
  • The last day. 8.1% of all payments (40.7 per alert) fall in the last day before the alert date, and 96.6% of these are stamped 23:57 to 23:59. Serious alerts have 42.0 of them, false alarms 40.4 (single-clue ROC-AUC 0.515). On every other day the 24 hours are equally used (4.15% to 4.18% of payments per hour). We treat both as side effects of how the data was generated.
  • Direction labels. "Card, in" (karta, kirim) is the most common kind, about 204 payments per customer, which is unusual if kirim means money coming in. In SynthAML the matching label marks payments that lower the balance. We use the labels as given; the model does not depend on their meaning.
  • Sizes are a standard index (miqdor_indeksi), not money amounts, so we only compare sizes with each other.
  • 178 training customers have no outgoing bank transfers (14.6% of them serious). They are not part of the chart in Section 4.

Method

Pipeline at a glance
  1. Load the alert files (CSV) and payment files (Parquet) with pandas and pyarrow.
  2. Build 113 clues from each alert's own 180-day history: counts and sizes per kind of payment, 30-day blocks, the last 7/30/90/180 days, time between payments and more.
  3. Learn the average payment size for each day before the alert (0 to 180) from training payments only, and subtract it from every payment (the "fix" from Section 3).
  4. Build 95 size-profile clues per kind of payment from the corrected sizes.
  5. Model: median imputation → standardization → logistic regression (L2, C = 0.002), all in one scikit-learn pipeline.
  6. Test with 5-fold stratified cross-validation (seed 42).
  7. Fit on all 14,000 training alerts and predict the 6,000 test alerts.
  8. Check the file (6,000 rows, one per test ID, no repeats, all values between 0 and 1) and save team_1B8F4334.csv.

Model settings and how we chose them

Logistic regression (scikit-learn, lbfgs solver, L2 penalty, no class weights, since ROC-AUC depends only on the order). Missing values (for example, a customer with no cash payments) are filled with the median, and every clue is standardized.

The most important setting is C, which controls how careful the model is: a smaller C keeps the points small unless the data strongly supports them. Cross-validated ROC-AUC for each value:

The score rises as the model becomes more careful, up to C ≈ 0.001–0.002 (equal within noise), then falls again.

How we avoided leaking answers
  • The size correction uses no labels, and it is learned from training payments only, then applied to the test payments.
  • Every clue for an alert comes only from that alert's own payments. Training and test share no alerts and no payments.
  • Imputation and standardization are fitted inside each cross-validation fold (one scikit-learn pipeline), so the held-out fold never influences them.
  • All choices were made with cross-validation on the training data. The test data was used once, for the final predictions.
  • No outside data or labels were used. The SynthAML paper was read only to understand the data.

Comparison with published research

The structure of this data closely matches SynthAML, a computer-made dataset built from a Danish bank's records (Jensen et al., 2023, Scientific Data). Its authors report these results for the same task:

Their 56-clue recipe scores 0.629 on the competition data, so our clues add a real improvement. On the real bank data they report about 0.75, which suggests that computer-made data like this limits what any model can reach.

What we tried that did not help

Each of these separated the groups only a little on its own (single-clue ROC-AUC 0.50 to 0.54) and added nothing once the size clues were in the model:

  • money going out within 1 to 24 hours after it comes in;
  • repeated, almost identical amounts and long runs of transfers;
  • night-time and weekend activity (expected, since the time of day is spread evenly in this data);
  • bursts of activity, including the last-day payments (single-clue ROC-AUC 0.515).

We also tried automatic clue selection (elastic net), a separate size correction for each kind of payment, a score for every single payment, and mixes of boosted trees with logistic regression. None improved the score by more than the noise between runs (about ±0.002), so we kept the simplest version.

Limitations
  • The data is computer-made. For the matching SynthAML data, models reach about 0.75 on the real bank records but about 0.64 on the synthetic version, so a ceiling near 0.64 is likely here too.
  • The differences between the groups are small. The model improves the order but gives no certainty: even in the top tenth of our queue, 71% of alerts are false alarms.
  • If kirim and chiqim are reversed, our plain-language "in" and "out" descriptions would swap, but the model and its score would not change.
  • The last-day payments look like a side effect of how the data was made; a real system would need to check how alerts are triggered.
  • A logistic regression does not model combinations of clues. Boosted trees, which can, did not do better on this data.

Reproducibility

How to reproduce our submission
  1. Open team_1B8F4334.ipynb in Google Colab or Jupyter (Python 3 with pandas, numpy, scikit-learn and pyarrow).
  2. Put fintech_track_data.zip next to the notebook. The first cell unzips it.
  3. Run all cells. It takes a few minutes and prints the cross-validation score (0.6398).
  4. The last cell checks the competition rules and writes team_1B8F4334.csv with 6,000 rows.

The random seed is fixed (42). We ran the notebook again from scratch in Google Colab and got the same predictions (differences below 10−14) and exactly the same ranking.
